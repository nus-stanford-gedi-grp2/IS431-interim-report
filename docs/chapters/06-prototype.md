## 6. Prototype Development {#prototype-development}

### Playbook rationale and structure {#playbook-development}

#### Why a playbook, and not a better kiosk {#why-playbook}

Our hotel visits (Chapter 5) surfaced six recurring problems with kiosks in practice: basic functionality (passport scan errors, bookings that fail to link, payment failures, jammed or missing key cards), clunky UI/UX, cabling, poor positioning, difficulty handling large tasks such as multi-room bookings, and a lingering demand for human touch. The natural reaction is to design a better kiosk. We chose not to, for three reasons:

1. **Kiosk design is a minor factor.** The gap between 20% usage at one hotel and 85% at another was driven largely by factors outside the machine: placement, visibility, staff training, guest fit and reliability. A better kiosk would leave most of that gap untouched.
2. **Fixing one pain point is not enough.** A tool that rectified a single hotel's current problem would not prevent the next manager from making different mistakes. We would need to address every pain point, across properties that serve very different guests.
3. **Hotels differ.** Luxury, mid-range and budget hotels, and hotels with different guest mixes, face different trade-offs. The valuable thing to hand over was not a single answer but a way of reaching the right answer for each property.

Our answer was therefore **not to build the best kiosk in the world, but to help managers understand how to implement kiosks well**: the strategy behind them, how they are operated, and what to do and avoid.

#### Deciding the form of the prototype {#playbook-requirements}

We began by asking two questions: *how do we help hotels make better decisions during kiosk implementation?* and *how do we create a guide that helps them handle the nuances of integrating a kiosk, not just designing one?* From these we set out the requirements for the prototype.

- **Audience.** The primary reader is the hotel manager responsible for the decision, but consultants and kiosk vendors are also likely users, so the content has to work for all three.
- **Guided, not just informative.** Managers often do not know what they actually want from a kiosk (lower manpower cost? a smoother arrival? a more innovative brand?). The first job of the prototype is to help them clarify their objective, with later content adapting to it.
- **Interactive and personalised.** Each stage should include an activity or template for the reader to complete, such as a guest persona or goal-setting exercise, with the reader's own values driving the results. This helps them move from theory to action.
- **Covers the full journey.** It should address strategy, design and execution so that no stage is left unsupported, and should include a cost-benefit analysis so managers can build a financial case for their owners.
- **Usable beyond the session.** Completed work should be exportable so that managers can take it into discussions with owners, vendors or funding agencies.

A static report or checklist would meet only the first of these. An interactive web-based **playbook** meets all of them, which is why we chose it as the Phase I prototype.

#### Structure: Strategy, Design, Execution {#playbook-structure}

The playbook follows three stages, mirroring how a hotel moves from deciding on a kiosk to running one:

| Stage | Question it answers | What the manager does |
|---|---|---|
| **Strategy** | Is a kiosk right for us, and what do we want from it? | Define objectives and set measurable goals; profile their guests; assess kiosk suitability; run a cost-benefit analysis (staff hours released, adoption scenarios, payback) |
| **Design** | What should the kiosk and its setting look like? | Apply principles for placement, physical and inclusive design, UI/UX, human–kiosk handoff, security and privacy, and guest fit; compare candidate placements using the Kiosk Placement Studio |
| **Execution** | How do we deploy and run it? | Plan product, technology, people, process and rollout workstreams; prepare for failure and low usage; plan for maintenance and improvement |

The **cost-benefit analysis** is built as a five-stage workflow: diagnose the current state, size the addressable demand, quantify the operational impact, monetise it, and decide. It distinguishes measured data from assumptions, tests low, base and high adoption scenarios, and treats staff hours released as redeployment capacity unless they can genuinely reduce shifts or headcount. Managers can also use it to obtain an initial figure for an STB grant application.

#### Validation {#playbook-validation}

We tested the playbook with Mr Sherwin Poh, Senior Manager (Operations) at The Ridge at NUS. He said it would have helped him make more informed considerations when planning his own deployment, and that the kiosk suitability rating could help win buy-in from superiors. Chapter 7 presents this feedback, and the limitations we identified, in full. The playbook is hosted as a website and completed outputs can be exported to PDF.
